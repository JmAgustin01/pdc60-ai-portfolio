A team escape game run by an AI game master: zero-shot, few-shot and role prompts, then RTCFC, COSTAR and RACE.

Team Greek AI: John Michael Agustin, Jerry Busa Jr. and Prince Emmanuel Lee, BSIT 4-A.

The game

A corrupted instruction sealed the “Experimental Prompt Vault.” Our team had 90 minutes to get through six locks and a final vault. The game master was ARGUS, an AI running inside a single free ChatGPT conversation from one master prompt. ARGUS tested our prompts, released puzzles, tracked a token wallet and kept an append-only ledger of every message.

The catch: a token budget

We started with 1,500 estimated input tokens. Every prompt, question, correction and unlock guess cost tokens, and a hint cost 40. Short, precise prompts weren't just good style, they were how you survived.

The six locks

Each door had two phases: a system test, where ARGUS ran our prompt on hidden inputs and checked the output against gate criteria, then a puzzle to actually open the door.

LockPrompting skillWhat our prompt had to do
1 · Blank-SlateZero-shotClassify campus help tickets into ACCESS, NETWORK, HARDWARE or OTHER, with no examples and a reason of five words or fewer.
2 · PatternFew-shotUse at least two accurate demonstrations so a new ticket gets exactly NETWORK | MULTIPLE USERS | ESCALATE OUTAGE.
3 · IdentityRole-basedAudit a bot that asked for a password, using a meaningful role (like a conversational UX auditor), in a FINDING | RISK | FIX table.
4 · Five ComponentsRTCFCA five-turn password-recovery dialogue that never asks for passwords and escalates safely.
5 · Six SignalsCOSTARAn 80 to 100 word maintenance announcement for first-year students, using only the given facts.
6 · Four CommandsRACEA four-stage recovery ladder for a frustrated user: rephrase, narrow, offer control, escalate to a human.
Final · Master VaultAll combinedA campus IT triage prompt with a role, few-shot examples and one named framework, passing four hidden tests.

What I took from it

  • Gate criteria are like a rubric for prompts. ARGUS judged the actual output, not whether the prompt claimed to include something. A prompt that says “be safe” but produces a password request still fails.
  • Few-shot examples must be consistent. One badly formatted demonstration teaches the model the wrong pattern.
  • Safety rules belong in the prompt. Several locks failed automatically if the output asked for passwords or invented links.
  • Constraints save tokens. A clear format and limit up front meant fewer retries, and retries were what drained the wallet.
